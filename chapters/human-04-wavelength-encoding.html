27.1 Human wavelength encoding overview

Vision scientists have learned a great deal about the first stages of vision. We have excellent measurements of how the eye’s optics (the cornea and lens) transform light from the world into an image on the retina, and we know how the retina’s photoreceptors encode that image into neural signals. This knowledge serves as a practical guide for designing any system that reproduces images for people.

Consider color. A camera designed for human viewing should capture the same portion of the electromagnetic spectrum that we see: visible light. Capturing less of the spectrum would lead to poor color reproduction. Capturing more—like infrared or ultraviolet—would be wasteful, consuming resources to record information we can’t see anyway. Worse yet, if we add in these signals into a reproduction the appearance would fail to match what we see. Our knowledge of human wavelength sensitivity sets a clear target for an image system designed for consumer photography.

Spatial resolution presents a different challenge. It would be a disappointment if a camera failed to capture details as fine as those the human eye can resolve. We expect our photos to be at least as sharp as what we see. However, unlike color, people are generally pleased if a camera captures a scene at a finer resolution than the eye encodes. We zoom in on the digital image to see details we might have missed. This extra information doesn’t interfere with the viewing experience; it just requires more storage and processing power.

So, we have two different engineering constraints derived from human vision:

  • Wavelength: Human vision sets a target range.
  • Spatial Resolution: Human vision sets a minimum bar.

In this section, we will review the way our visual system encodes wavelength and spatially samples the image. Understanding the human visual system properties is good guidance for what we cannot see. This is essential knowledge for designing image systems intended for consumer photography.

27.2 Color matching background

Thomas Young, giving the Bakerian lectures at the Royal Society in 1802 (Young 1802), directly explained why we should think that the 3-dimensionality of color -which was widely used in industry- was a property of the eye. But in the following years serious scientists, such as Brewster and Helmholtz, still made significant errors. Brewster argued that light was of reduced dimensionality (Brewster 1834). Helmholtz accepted that the idea that biology was essential, but a flawed experimental analysis made him think that there are five, not three, color receptors in the eye (Helmholtz 1852)1.

Young, Fresnel, and Maxwell

When Young delivered his Bakerian Lectures (1801–1803), he introduced two propositions that challenged British orthodoxy: first, that light travels as an undulatory wave governed by interference; and second, that human color vision is trichromatic because the retina possesses three distinct sensor mechanisms. The first was a resurrection of Huygens’ idea that the establishment had rejected. The second was a novel insight about color perception that flowed naturally from the first.

Both ideas were met with fierce resistance. As recounted in Section 8.4, Henry Brougham’s scathing broadsides in the Edinburgh Review battered Young’s reputation, contributing to his withdrawal from public optical debate for more than a decade.

Young’s legacy was fully restored through two subsequent masters of physics:

  1. Wave Optics (Fresnel): In France, Augustin-Jean Fresnel supplied the mathematical apparatus and experimental rigor that proved Young’s wave theory correct, collaborating with Young on transverse wave vibrations and winning the decisive triumph of wave optics over Newtonian corpuscles (Section 8.4).
  2. Color Vision (Maxwell): Decades later, James Clerk Maxwell supplied the quantitative colorimetry and mathematical framework that proved Young’s trichromatic hypothesis. Maxwell explicitly credited Young with discovering the biological basis of trichromacy—the insight that three-dimensionality is not an inherent property of physical light, but a constraint imposed by three classes of photoreceptors in the human eye.

Just as Fresnel did the heavy mathematical and experimental lifting that vindicated Young’s wave theory of light, Maxwell provided the experimental and theoretical foundation that supported Young’s biological theory of color vision. Through their work, Young’s insights became foundations of modern science.

My enthusiasm for Maxwell as a color scientist is based on the fact that (a) he settled, once and for all, the question of the biological basis of trichromacy, and (b) he did so in a way that established a clear conceptual structure and experimental methodology that we still use today(Maxwell 1857a; Maxwell 1860). Maxwell then collaborated with Thomas Sutton to produce the first color photographs Figure 1.4, demonstrating that his understanding was true and useful (Maxwell 1861; Sutton 1861). 2, though the significance of that color demonstration is controversial (Klein and Elter 2023).

Maxwell did the color work just prior to taking on the challenges of unifying the equations for electicity and magnetism and discovering that light is also electromagnetic radiation. I am so pleased that color science can call Maxwell one of our own.

27.3 Maxwell’s color matching experiments

Thomas Young’s suggestion that three-dimensional color vision is of biological origin was just that, a suggestion. It was based on his belief that light comprised waves and that the frequency of these waves was a continuous variable. He was aware that color could be reproduced using only three primary colors. To reconcile the continuous representation of light with the low dimensional representation of color, he wrote

Now, as it is almost impossible to conceive each sensitive point of the retina to contain an infinite number of particles, each capable of vibrating in perfect unison with every possible undulation, it becomes necessary to suppose the number limited for instance to the three principal colours … (Young 1802).

But even apart from the scurrilous attacks from Brougham, serious scientists still held on to the idea that light itself was three-dimensional (Brewster 1834), or that color vision had more than three dimensions (Helmholtz 1852). It was James Clerk Maxwell who developed the experiments and conceptual framework that settled the matter.

First, concerning the question of the dimensionality of the signal, Brewster had proposed that although light comnprised multiple wavelengths in fact there were only three fundamental types of lights -red,yellow,blue- that were distributed across the spectrum. Maxwell performed a simple experiment. Maxwell used his spinning color top to create metameric matches (for example, matching a combination of Vermilion, Emerald Green, and Ultramarine in the outer ring against a mixture of Chrome Yellow, Black, and White in the inner circle). He then viewed the metamers through a color filter. If the lights were the same - as predicted by the idea that we perceive them as the same because they are the same - then when the lights pass through the color filter they should still be the same (Maxwell 1857b). But,

The tints which before appeared identical will now be manifestly different, and will require alteration, to reduce them to equality. (maxwell-experiments?).

So the signal is not three-dimensional, and Young must have got it right.
