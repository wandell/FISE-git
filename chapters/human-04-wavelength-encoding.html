27.1 Human wavelength encoding overview

Vision scientists have learned a great deal about the first stages of vision. We have excellent measurements of how the eye’s optics (the cornea and lens) transform light from the world into an image on the retina, and we know how the retina’s photoreceptors encode that image into neural signals. This knowledge serves as a practical guide for designing any system that reproduces images for people.

Consider color. A camera designed for human viewing should capture the same portion of the electromagnetic spectrum that we see: visible light. Capturing less of the spectrum would lead to poor color reproduction. Capturing more—like infrared or ultraviolet—would be wasteful, consuming resources to record information we can’t see anyway. Worse yet, if we add in these signals into a reproduction the appearance would fail to match what we see. Our knowledge of human wavelength sensitivity sets a clear target for an image system designed for consumer photography.

Spatial resolution presents a different challenge. It would be a disappointment if a camera failed to capture details as fine as those the human eye can resolve. We expect our photos to be at least as sharp as what we see. However, unlike color, people are generally pleased if a camera captures a scene at a finer resolution than the eye encodes. We zoom in on the digital image to see details we might have missed. This extra information doesn’t interfere with the viewing experience; it just requires more storage and processing power.

So, we have two different engineering constraints derived from human vision:

  • Wavelength: Human vision sets a target range.
  • Spatial Resolution: Human vision sets a minimum bar.

In this section, we will review the key properties of the eye that define these limits. The way our visual system encodes wavelength, and the way it spatially samples the image, both have implications for how we design an image system. Understanding the human visual system properties is not explanation of what we see; it is good guidance for what we cannot see. This is essential knowledge for designing image systems for consumer photography.

27.2 Color matching principles

The most important concepts of color encoding were beautifully explained and tested in experiments by James Clerk Maxwell (Maxwell 1857; Maxwell 1860). He did this work just prior to taking on the challenges of unifying the equations for electicity and magnetism, and thus discovering that light is also electromagnetic radiation. I am so pleased that color science can call Maxwell one of our own1.

In the decades prior to Maxwell, there was considerable uncertainty as to whether our color percepts reflected properties of the physics of light or rather that they were imposed by the properties of the eye. Thomas Young, giving the Bakerian lectures at the Royal Society in 1802 (Young 1802), directly explained why we should think that the 3-dimensionality of color -which was widely used in industry- was a property of the eye. But even serious scientists, such as Brewster, continued to argue that light itself was of reduced dimensionality (Brewster 1834). Helmholtz accepted that the idea that biology was essential, but his initial writings were based on flawed analyses that suggested there were five, not three, color receptors in the eye.

Color appearance: Biology or physics

Young’s writings were severely criticized by Henry Brougham in Edinburgh Review, an ardent admirer of Newton, criticizing Thomas Young’s Bakerian Lectures.

…this paper contains nothing which deserves the name, either of experiment or of discovery, and as it is, in fact, destitute of every species of merit. We now dismiss, for the present, the feeble lucubrations of this author, in which we have searched without success for some traces of learning, acuteness, and ingenuity, that might compensate his evident deficiency in the powers of solid thinking, calm and patient investigation …

Young (1804) replied

A MAN who has a proper regard for the dignity of his own character, although his sensibility may sometimes be awakened by the unjust attacks of interested malevolence, will esteem it ingeneral more advisable to bear, in silence, the temporary effects of a short lived injury, than to suffer his own pursuits to be interrupted, in making an effort to repel the invective, and to punish the aggressor.
