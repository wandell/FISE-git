- Wavelength encoding

27.1 Human wavelength encoding overview

Vision scientists have learned a great deal about the first stages of vision. We have excellent measurements of how the eye’s optics (the cornea and lens) transform light from the world into an image on the retina, and we know how the retina’s photoreceptors encode that image into neural signals. This knowledge serves as a practical guide for designing any system that reproduces images for people.

Consider color. A camera designed for human viewing should capture the same portion of the electromagnetic spectrum that we see: visible light. Capturing less of the spectrum would lead to poor color reproduction. Capturing more—like infrared or ultraviolet—would be wasteful, consuming resources to record information we can’t see anyway. Worse yet, if we add in these signals into a reproduction the appearance would fail to match what we see. Our knowledge of human wavelength sensitivity sets a clear target for an image system designed for consumer photography.

Spatial resolution presents a different challenge. It would be a disappointment if a camera failed to capture details as fine as those the human eye can resolve. We expect our photos to be at least as sharp as what we see. However, unlike color, people are generally pleased if a camera captures a scene at a finer resolution than the eye encodes. We zoom in on the digital image to see details we might have missed. This extra information doesn’t interfere with the viewing experience; it just requires more storage and processing power.

So, we have two different engineering constraints derived from human vision:

  • Wavelength: Human vision sets a target range.
  • Spatial Resolution: Human vision sets a minimum bar.

In this section, we will review the way our visual system encodes wavelength and spatially samples the image. Understanding the human visual system properties is good guidance for what we cannot see. This is essential knowledge for designing image systems intended for consumer photography.

27.2 Color matching background

Thomas Young, giving the Bakerian lectures at the Royal Society in 1802 (Young 1802), directly explained why we should think that the 3-dimensionality of color -which was widely used in industry- was a property of the eye. But in the following years serious scientists, such as Brewster and Helmholtz, still made significant errors. Brewster argued that light was of reduced dimensionality (Brewster 1834). Helmholtz accepted that the idea that biology was essential, but a flawed experimental analysis made him think that there are five, not three, color receptors in the eye (Helmholtz 1852)1.

The trials and redemption of Thomas Young

When Thomas Young delivered his landmark Bakerian Lectures to the Royal Society between 1801 and 1803 (Young 1802, 1804a), he boldly introduced two ideas that fundamentally challenged orthodoxy: first, that light travels as an undulatory wave governed by the principle of interference; and second, that the presence of a continuous spectrum of waves means human color vision must be trichromatic because the retina possesses three distinct sensor mechanisms, not because the physical signal itself is trichromatic.

At the time, the British scientific establishment was fiercely devoted to Sir Isaac Newton’s corpuscular (particle) theory of light and opposed the wave theory. To question Newton was seen not merely as a scientific disagreement, but as an act of national and intellectual heresy.

Enter Henry Brougham, a dogmatic disciple of Newton’s corpuscular framework and co-founder of the influential Edinburgh Review, who launched a broadside against Young in its pages with a series of caustic, personal attacks:

…this paper contains nothing which deserves the name, either of experiment or of discovery, and as it is, in fact, destitute of every species of merit. We now dismiss, for the present, the feeble lucubrations2 of this author, in which we have searched without success for some traces of learning, acuteness, and ingenuity, that might compensate his evident deficiency in the powers of solid thinking, calm and patient investigation …

Brougham treated Young’s undulatory hypothesis—which revived ideas first championed by Christiaan Huygens—as a betrayal of Newton’s legacy. An amateur scientist, he spent substantial effort attempting to force diffraction into a corpuscular model of particles deflecting around obstacles. Being in an intellectual cult is something we should all try to avoid.

Young was a gentleman scholar and a practicing London physician. In 1804, he published a dignified pamphlet in his own defense—A Reply to the Animadversions of the Edinburgh Reviewers (Young 1804b):

A MAN who has a proper regard for the dignity of his own character, although his sensibility may sometimes be awakened by the unjust attacks of interested malevolence, will esteem it in general more advisable to bear, in silence, the temporary effects of a short-lived injury, than to suffer his own pursuits to be interrupted, in making an effort to repel the invective, and to punish the aggressor.

Young printed 1,000 copies of his defense at his own expense. Reportedly, only a single copy was sold. Meanwhile, the Edinburgh Review was circulating to thousands of Britain’s educated elite.

In Regency London, a physician accused in print of flighty, ungrounded speculation faced professional ruin; patients would not trust their health to an “unstable” theorist. Young’s authority was badly shaken, and he resigned his professorship of Natural Philosophy at the Royal Institution. Deeply bruised and isolated, Young largely withdrew from public optical debate for more than a decade. He turned his extraordinary polymathic intellect elsewhere: deciphering Egyptian hieroglyphs on the Rosetta Stone, writing medical treatises, and developing insurance mathematics.

More than 200 years later, this still pisses me off.

History was not finished with Thomas Young. In the twilight of the Napoleonic Wars (around 1814–1815), a young French civil engineer named Augustin-Jean Fresnel began experimenting on diffraction in Normandy. Cut off from British journals by war, and with Young’s papers buried by Brougham’s derision, Fresnel independently rediscovered the wave nature and interference of light.

When François Arago reviewed Fresnel’s findings at the Académie des Sciences in Paris, he realized that an English physician had anticipated many of these principles more than a decade earlier. Rather than sparking nationalistic rivalry, Arago and Fresnel reached out to Young. Fresnel openly credited Young with discovering the principle of interference, initiating a warm and generous correspondence.

The exchange bore extraordinary fruit. When Arago and Fresnel discovered that light beams polarized at right angles do not interfere—a puzzle impossible to explain if light waves were longitudinal, like sound—Young had another crucial leap of intuition in 1817: he suggested that light waves might be transverse, oscillating perpendicular to the direction of propagation, like the ripples on a plucked string (I discuss the physics of transverse waves and polarization in Section 8.5).

Fresnel embraced Young’s insight and built the rigorous mathematical mechanics of transverse waves. His equations beautifully accounted for polarization, double refraction, and diffraction. When the staunch Newtonian judge Siméon Denis Poisson argued that Fresnel’s equations led to the “absurd” consequence that a bright spot must appear in the very center of a circular shadow, Arago promptly conducted the experiment and observed the bright spot (the famous Poisson/Arago spot recounted in Section 8.4), handing wave theory a decisive victory over corpuscles.

Young’s vindication was complete. His detractors were silenced, not by polemics, but by the mathematical and experimental triumph of the theory he had pioneered. The mutual respect between Young and Fresnel became one of the most generous partnerships in the history of science. Young wrote of Fresnel:

Fresnel earned his success by his labor; he took the theory up where I left it, and carried it on to a degree of perfection to which I could never have brought it.3

In 1827, the Royal Society awarded Fresnel its highest honor, the Rumford Medal. As Foreign Secretary of the Royal Society, Young had the bittersweet privilege of writing to inform Fresnel of the prize. Fresnel, dying of tuberculosis at thirty-nine, received the medal just days before his death. He wrote back that receiving the esteem of Young and his peers was far dearer to him than any fleeting fame.

More on this historical episode can be found in Nahum’s analysis.

My enthusiasm for Maxwell is based on the fact that he settled, once and for all, the question of the biological basis of trichromacy. Moreover, he did so in a way that established a clear conceptual structure that we still use today(Maxwell 1857; Maxwell 1860). Beyond theory, he collaborated with Thomas Sutton to produce the first color photographs Figure 1.4, demonstrating that his understanding was true and useful (Maxwell 1861; Sutton 1861)4.

Maxwell did the color work just prior to taking on the challenges of unifying the equations for electicity and magnetism and discovering that light is also electromagnetic radiation. I am so pleased that color science can call Maxwell one of our own.
